The idea behind every agent swarm is more than two centuries old. Condorcet showed that a jury of mediocre voters becomes reliable as it grows, as long as each voter errs independently. Agent swarms rarely meet that condition.

When errors are correlated, a crowd of N judges is worth fewer than N. If {rho} is the average correlation between two members' errors, the crowd carries the information of

{neff} = N / (1 + (N − 1){rho})

independent judges. Put numbers in. With 1,000 agents and {rho} = 0.25, {neff} is 4. At 0.5 it's 2. Even at a correlation of 0.05, which sounds negligible, 1,000 agents are worth about 20.

The ceiling

As N grows, {neff} approaches 1/{rho} and stops. At {rho} = 0.25, no number of agents gets you past four. The only way up is to lower {rho}.

Where this shows up

In our own first experiment the agent crowd was four times the size of the human one and still lost to it (Echoes of Humanity, AMCIS 2026). The agents came from a handful of models and differed mostly by sampling temperature, so there were far fewer independent judges than the head count suggested.

Others see the same thing. Lefort and colleagues applied the Condorcet theorem to LLM ensembles and found only marginal gains. A 2026 preprint by Kohli, not yet refereed, reports nine-model judge panels behaving like roughly two independent votes.

Why it's the first thing we measure

{neff} is cheap to compute from outputs you already have, and it changes decisions. If your panel of twelve judges is worth two, the next dollar shouldn't go to judge thirteen.
