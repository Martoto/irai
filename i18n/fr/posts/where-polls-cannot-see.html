A persona model that tries to beat national polls on the headline number will lose, and winning would prove little. National polling in Brazil is dense. A model with no campaign information can only repeat what its training data says about Brazilians, and a model fed the news mostly hands the polls back.

So Model Society aims where polls are blind. Brazil has 5,570 municipalities and around 470,000 voting sections. The electoral authority publishes the electorate profile for every section and the result of every voting machine. That is thousands of out-of-sample checks per election.

What the model is allowed to say

We keep three things apart. The population frame comes from TSE registration data and the IBGE census. Turnout comes from past behaviour. The language models supply one quantity only: the probability that a voter of a given demographic type chooses a given option. Post-stratification then carries those probabilities down to any level where the population mix is known. Most silicon-sampling studies blend these parts and can't tell afterwards which one failed.

Baselines fixed in advance

The test is whether the society predicts differential swing better than uniform swing, which is a strong baseline because municipal results barely move between elections. At the finest scale we add a non-LLM benchmark built from section composition and past cell-level vote rates. If the models can't beat it, we'll say so.

Every level also has a noise floor. A voting section of about 300 people varies by roughly 3.3 percentage points from individual idiosyncrasy alone. We score against that floor.

Rules

  • Forecasts are hashed and timestamped before the outcome exists.
  • Nothing is released before polls close.
  • Outputs are labelled as synthetic simulations.
  • Model lean and refusals are reported as bias in the instrument.
  • A null result gets written up.
