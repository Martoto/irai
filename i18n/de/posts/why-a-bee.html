The iraí is a stingless bee, Nannotrigona testaceicornis, found across Brazil. It's a few millimetres long and black with grey hairs, and it nests in hollow bricks, stone walls and tree cavities, which is why you can find it in cities. Its name is usually read from Tupi as “river of honey”.

The colony builds a short entrance tube from wax and resin. At nightfall the bees close it. At dawn they open it again, and guards gather around the tube through the day. Researchers who studied the behaviour describe it as a trade-off between a wide door for foraging traffic and a safe colony.

We work on the same trade-off. A swarm is useful because a lot flows through it, and that is also what makes it easy to attack. The other thing we took from the bee: a colony has builders, foragers and guards. Nobody would run one on a thousand copies of the same worker.

This site follows the bee. Between dusk and dawn in São Paulo, the entrance is sealed.
